A systematic review can be technically polished and still answer the wrong question. Before databases, screening software, or statistical models enter the process, the clinical uncertainty needs to be stated precisely enough to guide every later choice.

## The question defines the work

Population, intervention, comparator, and outcome frameworks are useful, but a checklist is not a substitute for judgment. A strong question also makes clear why the answer would matter, which decisions it could inform, and where the important uncertainty lies.

This early framing determines what counts as relevant evidence. It shapes eligibility criteria, the search strategy, the data that need to be extracted, and whether quantitative synthesis is appropriate at all.

## Method follows structure

Pairwise and network meta-analysis are not interchangeable displays of sophistication. Each depends on a particular evidence structure and a set of assumptions that need to remain clinically credible.

The method should therefore follow the question and the available evidence—not the other way around. Sometimes the most rigorous result is a pooled estimate. Sometimes it is a structured explanation of why pooling would mislead.

## Precision includes restraint

Good synthesis makes uncertainty visible. It distinguishes absence of evidence from evidence of absence, avoids conclusions broader than the included studies, and keeps statistical significance separate from clinical importance.

That restraint is not a weakness. It is part of making research dependable enough to be useful.
